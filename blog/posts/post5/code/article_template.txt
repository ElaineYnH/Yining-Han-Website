Living without a car can mean different things in a city. It may fit a household's location and preferences, or accompany a tight budget and limited transport options. **Where do high shares of households without vehicles overlap with lower incomes in Philadelphia?** This matters for deciding where to investigate transport affordability and service needs. A map of vehicle availability alone cannot distinguish those circumstances.

## Mapping the overlap

I join the Census Bureau's **2020–2024 ACS five-year estimates** to **2024 census-tract boundaries** using the 11-digit tract GEOID. Both cover Philadelphia County, which shares the city's boundaries. The map uses all **@@tracts@@ tracts**. These are small statistical areas, rather than named neighborhoods; five-year estimates describe a period, not a single day in 2024.

The no-vehicle share is households with **no vehicle available divided by all households** in each tract. The citywide estimate is **@@city_rate@@%** (@@city_no_vehicle@@ of @@city_households@@ households). I compare each tract with that share and the **city median household income of $@@city_income@@**, in 2024 dollars. “Lower income” means a tract median below this reference, not an official poverty classification. These benchmarks come from the county estimates, rather than averages of tract percentages or medians.

![Philadelphia tracts classified by no-vehicle share and median household income relative to citywide reference values. Gray areas lack usable income or have no households.](results/figures/car_free_income_map.png){#fig-map fig-alt="Map of Philadelphia. High no-vehicle shares and lower incomes overlap in parts of North and West Philadelphia; high no-vehicle shares and higher incomes also occur around Center City."}

*Sources: U.S. Census Bureau, ACS tables B08201 and B19013; 2024 cartographic boundaries. Boundaries are projected to NAD83 / UTM zone 18N (EPSG:26918); the scale bar is in kilometers.*

The red areas show high no-vehicle shares alongside lower incomes, concentrated across parts of North and West Philadelphia. Blue areas around Center City also have above-city no-vehicle shares, but higher tract incomes. Thus, geographically concentrated car-free living has more than one income context. The red areas are sensible places to investigate whether transport costs restrict access to work and services. Their classification does **not** establish that existing transit is inadequate.

Nearby destinations or transit could make car-free living attractive in some places; limited purchasing power could constrain choices elsewhere. Those are possible explanations, not mechanisms identified by this comparison.

## Limits and takeaway

There are **@@missing_tracts@@ unclassified tracts**, including **@@zero_household_tracts@@ with zero households**. They account for **@@missing_city_no_vehicle_share@@%** of city no-vehicle households. ACS estimates have sampling error, so tracts near a cutoff may change categories. A check using 30% without a vehicle and income below $60,000 still places **@@rounded_no_vehicle_share@@%** of city no-vehicle households in the overlap group; exact boundaries are sensitive to the rule. Tract medians also conceal household differences: this analysis cannot count low-income car-free households or establish why anyone lacks a vehicle. It contains no routes, travel times, or service-quality measures.

**The useful signal is the overlap of car-free living and lower area incomes.** Planners could use the red areas as a starting point for checking fares, service reliability, and access to jobs, then gather route-level and household evidence before choosing interventions. The map identifies where to investigate; it does not by itself establish which transport investment would work.
